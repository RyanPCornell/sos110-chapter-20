Introduction · Earth Day 1970

Policy Can Change Everything

  • Prior to the 1970s: no national policies governing air or water pollution; no endangered species protection.
  • Denis Hayes — a graduate student in Camas, WA — organized the first Earth Day, mobilizing 20 million Americans.
  • Earth Day directly spurred Congress and President Nixon to create landmark environmental protection laws within a few years.

20.1 · How Are We All Subject to Government Decisions?

Governments, Legitimacy, and Coercion

  • Policies: authoritative decisions such as laws, regulations, and court rulings guiding behavior. Our daily choices — transportation, energy, food — are all shaped by government policies.
  • Governments maintain authority primarily through legitimacy.
  • Coercion: the use or threat of force — is a last resort that often undermines legitimacy over time.
  • Ordinary citizens can shape policy through voting, organizing, demonstrating, and participating in public comment processes.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
20.2 · Why Is the Way a Government Is Organized Important?

Government Systems and Environmental Policy

Authoritarian
Power concentrated in one person or small group. Can mandate rapid environmental changes, as China did with car restrictions and industrial regulations.
Democratic
Public plays a significant role in decision-making. Policy changes are slower but more durable — built on public consensus.
Federal Systems
States or provinces have powers independent of the central government (US, Canada, India, Brazil). Environmental policy fragmented across levels.
Unitary Systems
Central government alone directs policy (France, China). More consistent national environmental standards but less local flexibility.

20.2 · Rapid Environmental Change in China

Centralized Power as an Environmental Tool

  • China owns 2 of the world's top 5 wind turbine manufacturers, the 6 largest solar panel makers, the largest lithium-ion battery producer, and subsidized 80 electric car companies — all through centralized government direction.
  • To improve air quality, China required car owners to leave their cars at home at least one day per week and prohibited elevator use for the first 3 floors.
  • These changes occurred quickly and unilaterally — without public input.
  • In 2019, China cut automobile subsidies to consolidate the market by forcing weaker companies to compete for survival.
  • The US Clean Power Plan (Obama, 2015) was repealed in 2017 under Trump; the Inflation Reduction Act (Biden, 2022) restored and expanded climate investment.

20.4 · Interest Groups and Lobbying in Environmental Policy

The Role of Power in Policy

  • Interest groups attempt to persuade government decision-makers through lobbying — a constitutionally protected form of political participation. Environmental and industry groups compete for influence.
  • Surveys of company executives: nearly half report taking environmental actions to align with mission or values — not only in response to regulation.
  • Campaign finance: large donations from fossil fuel interests shape the political landscape for environmental policy.
  • Citizens and advocacy organizations can counter industry influence through organizing, public comment processes, and electoral engagement.

20.6 · How Did US Environmental Policies Develop?

Silent Spring to the Modern EPA

  • Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962): credited with sparking public concern about pesticides and pollution — contributing directly to the creation of the EPA in 1970.
  • Before the 1970s: rapid post-war development and synthetic chemical use with no national pollution policy.
  • EPA established 1970. Clean Air Act (1970), Clean Water Act (1972), and Endangered Species Act (1973) followed in rapid succession.
  • Natural resource policy: the federal government owns 28% of US land — and controls resource use through national forests, parks, and BLM lands.
  • These policy areas — pollution control and natural resources — remain the two major pillars of US environmental law.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
20.6 · Pollution Control Policies — The Major Laws

The Foundation of US Environmental Law

Clean Air Act (1970)
Regulates air pollutants from mobile and stationary sources. Established national ambient air quality standards.
Clean Water Act (1972)
Regulates pollution discharges into US waters. Sets water quality standards for surface waters.
RCRA (1976)
Resource Conservation and Recovery Act — governs hazardous and solid waste generation, transport, and disposal.
CERCLA / Superfund (1980)
Comprehensive Environmental Response Act — funds cleanup of contaminated hazardous waste sites.

What Did Those Laws Actually Save?

Sources & fine print

Every number on this slide, cited

  1. US EPA (2011). The Benefits and Costs of the Clean Air Act from 1990 to 2020 (Second Prospective Study). Central estimate: the 1990 Amendments prevent ~230,000 adult premature deaths in the year 2020 alone; annual benefits ~$2.0 trillion vs. ~$65 billion compliance costs (~30:1; sensitivity range ~3:1–90:1). epa.gov/clean-air-act-overview
  2. US EPA (1997). The Benefits and Costs of the Clean Air Act, 1970 to 1990 (Retrospective Study). ~205,000 premature deaths avoided in 1990 alone; total 1970–1990 benefits $5.6–49.4 trillion (mean $22.2T) vs. ~$0.5 trillion costs.
  3. CDC NHANES / US EPA. After the Clean-Air-Act-driven phase-out of leaded gasoline, average blood-lead levels in young US children fell more than 90% from the late-1970s baseline (~15 µg/dL).
  4. Keiser, D.A. & Shapiro, J.S. (2019). “Consequences of the Clean Water Act and the Demand for Water Quality.” Quarterly Journal of Economics 134(1): 349–396. Most pollution measures fell sharply after 1972; the share of waters violating fishable standards declined ~12 percentage points; ~$650B (2014$) flowed through municipal treatment grants, ~$1T total abatement spending. They also note official benefit tallies omit most health and ecosystem values.
  5. US Fish & Wildlife Service (2020). Bald eagle population update: 417 known breeding pairs in the lower 48 in 1963 → an estimated 71,467 pairs by 2019. Bald eagle delisted 2007.
  6. Greenwald, N., Suckling, K.F., Hartl, B. & Mehrhoff, L. (2019). “Extinction and the U.S. Endangered Species Act.” PeerJ 7:e6803 — the ESA prevented an estimated 291 extinctions through 2019; more than 99% of listed species survive.

Honest caveats: benefit estimates rely on valuing statistical lives and are uncertain (hence the published ranges); Clean Water Act benefits are the hardest to price because drinking-water health effects and ecosystem services were largely excluded from official cost–benefit tallies.

20.7 · How Are Environmental Issues Addressed Internationally?

Sovereignty, IGOs, and International Law

  • Environmental impacts cross national borders — acid rain, ocean pollution, and greenhouse gases ignore political boundaries. International cooperation is essential, but sovereignty makes it difficult.
  • Intergovernmental organizations (IGOs): UNEP, WHO, WTO, formed by member countries to facilitate cooperation.
  • The IPCC convenes thousands of experts to assess climate science and inform policy, which is a model of evidence-based international governance.
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
20.7 · International Agreements — How They Work in the US

Treaties, Agreements, and the Senate

  • US treaties require 2/3 Senate ratification — a high bar that has prevented US participation in several international environmental agreements.
  • Executive agreements (not requiring Senate approval) can achieve some international commitments but are reversible by the next president (as seen with the Paris Agreement).
© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
20.7 · The Montreal Protocol — A Success Story

Why the Montreal Protocol Worked

  • The 1987 Montreal Protocol is considered the world's most successful international environmental agreement — the ozone layer is on track to recover by mid-century.
  • Scientific consensus was clear: CFCs deplete stratospheric ozone, increasing UV radiation and skin cancer.
  • Economic alternatives to CFCs already existed — making compliance relatively painless for industry.
  • Universal participation: 197 countries ratified, including all major CFC producers.
  • Lessons for climate policy: agreements succeed when the science is clear, alternatives are available, and costs are shared equitably.

20.5 / 20.8 · Connecting Policy to Action — Synthesis

Every Policy Was Once Someone's Idea

  • Denis Hayes was a graduate student when he organized Earth Day 1970. Policy change begins with citizens who care enough to act — and understand how the system works.
  • Environmental policy is not made by governments alone — citizens, advocacy groups, businesses, and courts all play roles.
  • Understanding the policy cycle (agenda setting → formulation → legitimation → implementation → evaluation) empowers participation at every stage.
  • The most effective environmental advocates combine scientific literacy with political knowledge and communication skills.
  • Your generation will inherit the policy landscape — and has the power to reshape it.

Round 1 has no pollution cap — polluting is free. Then a cap on pollution arrives and gets stricter every round. Watch total pollution fall — and watch each factory pick its cheaper path: clean up, go under the cap, and sell credits… or keep polluting over the cap and buy them.
Cap-and-trade: the regulator sets a pollution CAP per factory and lowers it over time. Emit UNDER your cap and you earn credits to sell; emit OVER it and you must buy credits from cleaner factories. Each factory picks whichever is cheaper — cleaning up or buying — so the cap's pollution cut happens at the lowest possible total cost.
